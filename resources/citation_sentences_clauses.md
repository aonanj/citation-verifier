# Citation Structures

**In-text or inline citation**: In non-academic legal documents, such as briefs and opinions, citations generally appear within the text of the document immediately following the propositions they support. 

**Footnote or endnote citations**: Footnotes generally appear in academic documents, but can appear in legal documents too. Generally have a number (e.g., `1.` or `1`) or symbol (e.g., `*`) prepended, corresponding to the same number elsewhere in the document. The footnote number is generally in superscript. 

Do not mix the two types of citations when extracting citations. Prioritize footnotes or endnotes over inline citations -- if a document has footnotes or endnotes, only extract those citations. 

## Citation Sentences and Clauses

A citation may be inserted into the text of a document in one of two ways: as a stand-alone citation sentence or as a citation clause.

- **Citation sentences** begin with a capital letter and end with a period. One citation sentence may contain multiple citations separated by semicolons. Citation sentences are used to cite sources and authorities that relate to the entire preceding sentence. Example excerpt showing inline citation sentences: 
    > "The U.S. Supreme Court has the power to invalidate statutes that are repugnant to the U.S. Constitution. Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177–79 (1803) (federal laws); Fletcher v. Peck, 10 U.S. (6 Cranch) 87, 139 (1810) (state laws); Dred Scott v. Sandford, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 393, 395 (1856), superseded by constitutional amendment, U.S. Const. amend. XIII. The U.S. Supreme Court also has the power to review state court decisions. Martin v. Hunter’s Lessee, 14 U.S. (1 Wheat.) 304, 377–78 (1816) (civil cases); Cohens v. Virginia, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 350 (1821) (criminal cases)."
    - The first sentence has 4 citations:
        - `Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177–79 (1803) (federal laws)`
        - `Fletcher v. Peck, 10 U.S. (6 Cranch) 87, 139 (1810) (state laws)`
        - `Dred Scott v. Sandford, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 393, 395 (1856)`
        - `U.S. Const. amend. XIII`
    - The second sentence has 2 citations:
        - `Martin v. Hunter’s Lessee, 14 U.S. (1 Wheat.) 304, 377–78 (1816) (civil cases)`
        - `Cohens v. Virginia, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 350 (1821) (criminal cases)`

- **Citation clauses** are set off from the text by commas and immediately follow the proposition to which they relate. Citation clauses do not begin with a capital letter unless the citation clause begins with a source that would otherwise be capitalized. Citation clauses are not ended with a period, unless it is the last clause in the sentence. Citation clauses to cite sources and authorities that relate to only part of a sentence. Example showing inline citation clauses:
    > "The Supreme Court adopted a broad reading of the Commerce Clause during the New Deal, see Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111, 128–29 (1942), though in recent years the Supreme Court has somewhat reined in its broad reading, see United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549, 624 (1995); United States v. Morrison, 529 U.S. 598, 612–13 (2000); Nat’l Fed’n of Indep. Bus. v. Sebelius, 567 U.S. 519, 552 (2012)."
    - This sentence has 4 citations: 
        - Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111, 128–29 (1942)
        - United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549, 624 (1995)
        - United States v. Morrison, 529 U.S. 598, 612–13 (2000)
        - Nat’l Fed’n of Indep. Bus. v. Sebelius, 567 U.S. 519, 552 (2012).
